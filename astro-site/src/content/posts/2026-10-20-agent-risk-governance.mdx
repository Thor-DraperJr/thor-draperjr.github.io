I almost wish we'd kept calling some of these things bots.

I've been trying to keep a working guideline for evaluating agent risk. The word "agent" covers a lot of ground, and some agent-builder experiences look to me like large prompt books: reusable instructions, selected knowledge, and a consistent way to ask for something.

Useful, absolutely. But if it still works within your permissions, answers when you ask, and leaves you responsible for taking action, how much new authority did we introduce?

Microsoft describes Agent Builder as respecting existing Microsoft 365 permissions, without granting new privileges.[8] Its current external-service action boundary also matters: adding those actions means moving to Copilot Studio.[6]

For that bounded pattern, I'd expect relatively little net-new authority. I want the governance process to reflect that. A reusable prompt book shouldn't inherit the release process for an autonomous worker just because both are called agents.

There is still risk in what people do with the answer. Microsoft's middle risk tier explicitly includes wrong answers that mislead people or disrupt operations.[1] Existing permissions also remain something to examine; preserving access doesn't tell you whether that access was appropriate in the first place.

This map is how I keep the different solutions and control boundaries straight.

The six categories and risk progression are my framework. Moving right, I'm thinking about expanding capability, connected systems, and authority. The shared governance foundation stays underneath all of it.

The product name is a starting point. Foundry itself supports configuration-driven and code-based agents.[4] I'd still evaluate the actual deployment before assigning risk.

## The risk changes when the authority changes

Microsoft's Center of Excellence guidance calls the assist-to-execute line its clearest risk signal. It describes three tiers:[1]

- **Individual productivity:** named owner, basic monitoring, release checklist, and self-service guardrails.
- **Expert knowledge and internal services:** domain validation, knowledge-quality monitoring, formal release review, and accuracy feedback.
- **Business-critical and external-facing:** process ownership, SLA monitoring, security and responsible AI review, decision rights, incident response, and quarterly maturity review.

Its citizen, partnered, and professional development zones add guidance on how teams build and manage agents.[2]

The platform notes under my map cover the separate identity, onboarding, and Autopilot implementation details.[5][3][7]

My first questions would be: whose permissions does it use, what can it change, and does a person approve the action?

Those answers tell me more than the agent label does.
